Data Collection

Motivation

Chronic diseases pose some of the greatest challenges to global health1. Unlike acute conditions, they develop gradually and persist for extended periods, often leading to serious complications, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality2. Their widespread impact extends beyond individuals, burdening healthcare systems and exacerbating global health inequities3. Conditions such as diabetes, heart disease, and hypertension are particularly concerning due to their rising prevalence and profound social and economic consequences.

Despite the vast amounts of health data collected by organizations like the World Health Organization (WHO), much of this data remains fragmented and underutilized4. Essential health indicators are often scattered across various sources, making it difficult to form a complete picture of the global burden of chronic diseases. This project was motivated by the need to bridge that gap by creating a unified dataset that integrates key chronic disease indicators.

Health data tells compelling stories. For example, a rising average BMI in a country could signal the onset of a diabetes epidemic5. An increase in hypertension prevalence might foreshadow a surge in heart attacks or strokes6. These indicators act like warning signals, flashing on a control panel—if monitored and interpreted correctly, they can guide timely public health interventions, inform resource allocation, and shape life-saving policies.

However, effective health policies require more than isolated statistics. They demand a comprehensive, data-driven understanding of how chronic diseases interact with demographic factors, geographic locations, and time trends. This project was driven by the belief that integrated data leads to deeper health insights. By building a comprehensive dataset from WHO’s publicly available statistics, I sought to explore the following questions:

  • How accurately can chronic disease risks be predicted based on health indicators like BMI, diabetes prevalence, and hypertension control?

  • Which features contribute the most to predicting chronic disease outcomes, and how do their impacts differ across models?

  • Can binary classification models effectively distinguish between low-risk and high-risk individuals?

  • How well can multiclass classification models categorize individuals into specific health risk levels based on integrated health data?

  • Can clustering methods uncover hidden patterns in chronic disease data, such as emerging risk groups not defined by existing health indicators?

Chronic diseases are more than just statistics—they affect real people, families, and communities. Addressing these persistent health challenges requires transforming global health data into actionable insights. This project aims to bridge the gap between health data and meaningful solutions by building and evaluating machine learning models that predict chronic disease risks using key indicators such as BMI, diabetes prevalence, and hypertension control.

The primary objective is to create a clean, well-preprocessed dataset by handling multicollinearity, selecting relevant features, and standardizing variables. Supervised learning models will be developed for binary classification to distinguish between low-risk and high-risk individuals, multiclass classification to categorize health risk levels, and regression analysis to predict continuous health outcomes. Unsupervised learning techniques like K-Means, DBSCAN, and Hierarchical Clustering will uncover hidden patterns, while PCA and t-SNE will enhance data visualization and interpretability.

Model evaluation will involve metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, R², and Silhouette scores. The best-performing models will be selected based on comparative analysis. Insights gained from this analysis will inform public health strategies, guide future research, and highlight critical features driving chronic disease risks. Through this project, I hope to uncover meaningful stories hidden within global health statistics, supporting efforts toward a healthier and more equitable world.

Methods

This project employed a comprehensive data science workflow, including data collection, preprocessing, analysis, and model evaluation. The key techniques, algorithms, and tools used in this process are outlined below:

Data Collection

Data was sourced from the World Health Organization (WHO)7, focusing on global health indicators such as BMI, diabetes prevalence, and hypertension statistics. The dataset was compiled by aggregating relevant health metrics across various countries and years to ensure broad coverage and meaningful insights.

Data Preprocessing

The preprocessing stage involved handling missing values, detecting and addressing multicollinearity, and standardizing numerical features using StandardScaler from sklearn. Categorical variables such as gender and health status were encoded using label encoding. Strongly correlated features like Diabetes_AgeStd and Hypertension_Treatment were dropped to prevent redundancy.

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

EDA was conducted using visualization libraries such as matplotlib, seaborn, and plotly. Key insights were derived from correlation matrices, descriptive statistics, and feature importance plots. These analyses guided feature selection and model design decisions.

Machine Learning Models

Supervised Learning

  • Binary Classification: Models like Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting were developed to distinguish between low-risk and high-risk individuals.
  • Multiclass Classification: The same models were extended to classify individuals into health risk levels (Low, Moderate, High).
  • Regression Analysis: Continuous prediction of diabetes prevalence was performed using models such as Linear Regression, Random Forest Regressor, and Gradient Boosting Regressor.

Unsupervised Learning

  • Dimensionality Reduction: PCA and t-SNE were applied for better visualization and interpretability of complex data structures.
  • Clustering: Techniques like K-Means, DBSCAN, and Hierarchical Clustering were used to uncover hidden patterns.

Model Evaluation

Performance metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, R², Silhouette scores, and confusion matrices were used to evaluate model performance. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using GridSearchCV to optimize model parameters.

This integrated approach ensured a robust, data-driven exploration of chronic disease risk factors, providing meaningful insights to inform public health strategies and future research.

WHO Data Collection

The WHO API provides access to a comprehensive database of global health indicators, covering various aspects of public health, including chronic disease prevalence, risk factors, and treatment coverage. Since querying the API requires specific indicator codes, the first step involved fetching the full list of indicators using the WHO Indicator API Endpoint. The data was saved as who_indicators_list.csv for further filtering.

Relevant indicators were identified by searching for keywords such as “blood,” “pressure,” “glucose,” “diabetes,” and “hypertension.” These terms align with the project’s focus on chronic disease analysis. After filtering, key indicators related to BMI, hypertension, and diabetes were selected based on their relevance to the research objectives. This streamlined dataset enabled targeted data collection using the WHO API.

The selected indicators were programmatically fetched using Python’s requests library and saved as CSV files. Each file represents a specific health indicator, structured with consistent columns for easy integration and analysis. These files include data on average BMI, diabetes prevalence, and hypertension control rates across countries, demographic groups, and years, forming the foundation for the project’s subsequent data analysis and modeling efforts.

Each CSV file corresponds to a specific health indicator :

  8. who_blood_glucose_crude.csv:
    • Mean fasting blood glucose levels, crude estimates, reflecting the average glucose concentration without adjustments.

Columns in the WHO Dataset

The WHO datasets contain a range of metadata, demographic details, numerical data, and additional context-specific information. Here’s a breakdown of the key columns found across the collected CSV files:

1. Metadata Columns:

  • Id: Unique identifier for each observation (Integer).
  • IndicatorCode: Unique code representing the health indicator (String).
  • SpatialDimType & SpatialDim: Geographic classification, indicating country or region (String).
  • ParentLocationCode & ParentLocation: Higher-level geographic grouping for regional aggregation (String).
  • TimeDimType & TimeDim: Temporal classification indicating the year or time period of the data (String & Integer).

2. Demographic Dimensions:

  • Dim1Type & Dim1: Primary demographic factor (e.g., gender, age group) (String).
  • Dim2Type & Dim2: Secondary demographic category, if applicable (String).
  • Dim3Type & Dim3: Tertiary demographic category, for further breakdown (String).

3. Data Source:

  • DataSourceDimType & DataSourceDim: Information about the data source or collection method (String).

4. Numerical Data Columns:

  • Value: Recorded health statistic, often provided as a descriptive range (String).
  • NumericValue: Central numerical value representing the indicator’s exact measurement (Float).
  • Low & High: Confidence interval bounds indicating the reliability of the reported statistic (Float).

5. Additional Columns:

  • Comments: Additional notes or remarks about the data entry (String).
  • Date: Date when the data was last updated (Datetime).
  • TimeDimensionBegin & TimeDimensionEnd: Start and end dates of the observation period, if applicable (Datetime).

These columns provide essential context and structure, supporting a comprehensive understanding of global health indicators, demographic patterns, and trends crucial for the analysis of chronic disease prevalence.

Summary

The project involved collecting and processing global health indicators from the WHO API, focusing on metrics such as BMI, diabetes prevalence, and hypertension control. Data retrieval required structured API requests, filtering, and cleaning to address missing records, duplicated entries, and inconsistent time-series formatting. A critical step in data preprocessing was handling multicollinearity through correlation analysis. Features such as Diabetes_AgeStd and Hypertension_Treatment were removed due to high correlations with similar indicators, reducing redundancy and streamlining the dataset for efficient model development.

Supervised learning models were developed for binary classification, multiclass classification, and regression using Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting. These models were optimized through hyperparameter tuning and evaluated with performance metrics such as accuracy, F1-score, R², and confusion matrices. Unsupervised learning techniques, including K-Means, DBSCAN, and Hierarchical Clustering, were applied to explore underlying data structures. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and t-SNE were employed for dimensionality reduction and improved visualization.

Several technical challenges emerged during the project. Data imbalance posed a significant issue, particularly in health risk categories, requiring evaluation through balanced accuracy and weighted metrics. Overfitting concerns were addressed through cross-validation, regularization, and model complexity control. Dimensionality reduction presented its own challenges, balancing computational efficiency with the need for interpretability.

Unexpected findings added valuable depth to the analysis. Clustering models like K-Means and DBSCAN revealed overlapping clusters, indicating that the chosen features may lack sufficient distinctiveness for clear segmentation. This result suggests the potential value of more advanced clustering methods such as Gaussian Mixture Models. The consistently high performance of Random Forest and Gradient Boosting models highlighted their effectiveness for chronic disease prediction tasks, despite the dataset’s complexity.

The project successfully integrated various health metrics into a unified dataset, demonstrating how machine learning can effectively predict chronic disease risks and uncover hidden health patterns. Moving forward, expanding the dataset with more comprehensive health metrics and demographic data from additional sources would enhance the analysis. Advanced modeling techniques, including deep learning and time-series forecasting, could improve predictive accuracy and interpretability. Automating the entire pipeline and deploying models in real-time monitoring systems would further extend the project’s impact, making it a scalable and actionable health prediction platform.
